\begin{abstract}
The fission matrix (FM) method enables whole-core reactor transport calculations within seconds by interpolating a precalculated database of Monte Carlo (MC)--tallied coefficients across different operational parameters instead of running a direct MC calculation for every configuration of interest. This work focuses on the parametrization of the FM with respect to the temperature distribution within a micro core. Interpolation implicitly assumes that each coefficient depends only on the local temperature of cells where fission occurs. This assumption breaks down under large, spatially sharp temperature gradients. In this work, a Core Temperature Correction Ratio (CTCR) is introduced to compensate this effect.

% A point-estimate improvement alone, however, cannot distinguish a real, uncorrected bias from the statistical noise inherent to the underlying MC calculations.
The results require an uncertainty budget with two parts: the MC statistics of the reference case, and the uncertainty of the eigenvalue ($k_{\text{eff}}$) and eigenvector ($\nu\Sigma_f\phi$) of the \emph{interpolated} matrix itself, a new object assembled from the database's finite-statistics matrices, which has no MC replicas of its own. We construct the latter by resampling the database and recomputing the interpolated (and corrected) result many times, comparing an uncorrelated baseline, \revC{an} analytical model derived in this work, a measured-correlation model, and a brute-force reconstruction from 150 independent replicas. Validated against brute force across 12 test cases, the analytical model reproduces the brute-force $k_{\text{eff}}$ uncertainty to within a mean absolute deviation of 4.6\%, matching or exceeding the two \revB{alternative resampling models}, while requiring no MC replicas beyond the nominal database already needed for interpolation. With both uncertainty sources combined, the CTCR improves the result in almost all cases: it removes most of the point-estimate $k_{\text{eff}}$ discrepancy for sharply varying, highly localized temperature fields, whereas the improvement is smaller (and less necessary) for smoother temperature distributions. The local fission-source error follows the same pattern, improving substantially wherever a strong, spatially localized temperature gradient is present; where the correction succeeds, the residual discrepancy is generally consistent with the combined statistical noise of the reference case and the interpolated database.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Monte Carlo (MC) methods provide the high-fidelity treatment of the neutron transport equation in reactor physics applications, at the cost of a statistical uncertainty that decreases only as the inverse square root of the simulated particle histories. Hybrid neutron transport methods based on the fission matrix (FM) formulation~\cite{CarneyANE2014} address this cost by precalculating a database of FM coefficients under a set of reference system configurations and then interpolating and combining these coefficients to represent an arbitrary configuration within the database's validity range. 

There are two different strategies for obtaining the FM of a single core configuration. The first one assembles the whole-core FM by running one fixed-source calculation for each ``fundamental cell'' of the system. As described in~\cite{WaltersNSE2018}, the fundamental units are identified by exploiting the geometrical and material similarities between repeated units to limit the required database calculations. Reflectors and structural materials are deliberately excluded from the FM tally to preserve this similarity, and their influence is recovered afterward through dedicated boundary and discontinuity corrections. This is the approach of RAPID~\cite{WaltersNSE2018}, a mature implementation yielding whole-core transport solutions within a few minutes at accuracy comparable to the underlying MC calculations, aided by dedicated techniques for FM homogenization~\cite{HePNE2020Homog}, temperature interpolation~\cite{HeANE2020Temp}, and correction of material and control-rod discontinuity effects~\cite{HeANE2019,HePNE2020,Mascolino2022}.
The present work adopts the second strategy to obtain the whole-core FM for a single configuration, which consists in tallying the FM elements directly from a single whole-core criticality calculation in Serpent~\cite{LeppanenEPJN2025}, so that reflectors, structural materials, and inter-assembly heterogeneity effects are already embedded in each coefficient, and a complete fission matrix is obtained from one calculation rather than assembled from fundamental-unit responses.
The selection of the most suitable approach is case-specific: it depends on the reactor design and on the purpose of the analysis, keeping in mind the features of the two methods. The method used in RAPID, in fact, requires, where applicable, the recognition of the fundamental cells of the system, and the definition of multiple fixed-source MC calculations to build up one single whole-core FM. To guarantee the right convergence of the eigen-problem's results of the resulting FM, it is important that the mesh of the single fixed-source cases is fine enough to capture all the local effects, at the same time a too small grid would be a burden memory-wise. This means that the dimension of the FM is not completely arbitrary but it must be a trade-off between the result-accuracy and the hard-disk storing capacity. Generally speaking the fixed-source calculations need less computational time and the uncertainty of the tallied FM elements is small if enough neutron histories are set. 
The whole-core FM deriving from a criticality calculation, instead, uses an already-converged neutron source, so that there is no direct dependence on the accuracy of the fundamental eigenvalue and eigenfunction. Because the coefficients are accumulated on the fly from a converged source, the within-cell spatial distribution of the fission source is represented much more accurately than with the fixed-source technique, so that much larger spatial cells can be used~\cite{WaltersNSE2018}. On the other hand, to obtain data with a low-enough uncertainty, especially for pin-wise cells, a high number of MC histories is necessary.             

The second method is chosen because the goal is a simple workflow giving reliable FM results for an online core-monitoring tool for the Westinghouse eVinci reactor, where pin-wise accuracy is not needed. 

The system studied here is a modified Heat-Pipe Micro-Reactor (HPMR) concept, and the operational variable of interest is the mesh cell temperature: the FM database is tallied at a set of uniform reference temperatures, and an arbitrary in-core temperature field is represented by interpolating each coefficient across that database. As a general approach widely adopted to deal with interpolations performed on temperature distributions, it is assumed that a coefficient $a_{ij}$ depends only on the \emph{local} temperature of the destination cell, i.e. the $i$-th cell. However, under a large, spatially sharp temperature gradient that assumption breaks down and the systematic error affecting the interpolated fission matrix can increase. To compensate it, we introduce a Core Temperature Correction Ratio (CTCR), calibrated once from two additional FMs, and show that it reduces the point-estimate discrepancy between interpolated and directly-simulated reference results across a range of test cases. \revC{The CTCR and this point-estimate comparison were first presented in a conference paper~\cite{VitaPHYSOR2026}, which the present paper extends with an estimate of the statistical uncertainty of the interpolated results.}

To better understand the systematic error and the improvement provided by the CTCR, a point-estimate comparison of the interpolated FMs and the reference one directly calculated with Serpent may not be enough to distinguish a real, uncorrected modeling bias, since both the database and the reference case are generated by Monte Carlo simulations, which always exhibit intrinsic statistical noise. Making that distinction requires quantifying the \revC{statistical} uncertainty on both quantities being compared. This estimation is straightforward for the reference case: Serpent~\cite{LeppanenEPJN2025} reports its own statistical uncertainty for each tallied quantity, including both $k_{\text{eff}}$ and the fission source, $F$. On the contrary, the results of the interpolated matrix are provided \revC{without} any uncertainty: the eigenvalue and eigenvector of the matrix produced by interpolation (and CTCR correction) are new objects and, unlike a direct MC run, have no replicas of their own from which an uncertainty could be read off. The second aim of this work is to fill this gap with an inexpensive uncertainty estimate for the interpolated FM. This will allow us to better quantify any systematic error and the improvement provided by the correction ratio.  
The paper describes the database, the interpolation and the CTCR, then the two uncertainty sources and the four ways of estimating the database-side one, the error metrics, and finally the test cases and their results.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{The Fission Matrix Method}
\label{sec:fm_method}

The fission matrix (FM) method~\cite{CarneyANE2014} recasts the $k$-eigenvalue transport problem into a compact linear-algebraic form by partitioning the fissile domain into $N$ spatial regions and describing the transport process among them over a averaged single neutron generation. Applying the integral form of the $k$-eigenvalue transport equation over the volumes of a source region $j$ and a destination region $i$ yields an algebraic eigenvalue problem,
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N} a_{ij}\,F_j,
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{F} = k\,\mathbf{F},
  \label{eqn:fm_eigen}
\end{equation}
where $F_j$ is the fission-neutron source integrated over region $j$, and the coefficient $a_{ij}$ encodes the transport process yielding the expected number of fission neutrons born in region $i$ per fission neutron emitted in region $j$. The $N\times N$ array of these coefficients is the fission matrix $\mathbf{A}$: its dominant eigenvalue is $k_{\text{eff}}$ and the associated eigenvector is the region-wise fission-source distribution~\cite{CarneyANE2014}. 
% Throughout this work the column index $j$ labels the \emph{source} region and the row index $i$ the \emph{destination} region --- a convention that underlies the correlation structure derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:analytical_derivation}.

Since each coefficient is defined per emitted source neutron, the fission multiplicity is already embedded in it: a source neutron that induces a fission contributes $\bar\nu$ neutrons to the destination region. A key practical feature of the method is that the coefficients can be accumulated at negligible additional cost during an ordinary Monte Carlo criticality calculation, by tallying, over the active cycles, the region of birth of each fission neutron against the region of birth of its parent. In this work the full fission matrix (one region per fuel assembly, yielding a $127\times127$ operator) is obtained in this way from a single whole-core Serpent calculation. Hence, every coefficient reflects the complete core environment.


\subsection{Database, Interpolation, and Correction Ratio}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Rather than tallying a fission matrix directly for every temperature field of interest, the method precalculates a database of fission matrices at a set of uniform reference temperatures and interpolates within it. To keep the method practical, the database must stay compact without losing accuracy; the one used here contains the coefficients $a_{ij}$ at only eight uniform temperatures spanning 300--1000~K in 100~K increments. For an arbitrary temperature field, each coefficient is first obtained by piecewise-linear interpolation between the two bracketing database temperatures:

\begin{equation}
  a_{ij}(T_i) = a_{ij}^{-} + \frac{T_i - T_i^{-}}{T_i^{+} - T_i^{-}} \left( a_{ij}^{+} - a_{ij}^{-} \right),
  \label{eqn:interp}
\end{equation}

where $a_{ij}^{-}$ and $a_{ij}^{+}$ are the values of $a_{ij}$ at the database temperatures immediately below and above the arbitrary destination-cell temperature $T_i$.

Eq.~(\ref{eqn:interp}) treats each coefficient as depending only on the local temperatures $i$, and therefore misses the influence of the surrounding cells. Under a large, spatially sharp gradient this is precisely where the interpolated matrix acquires a systematic error. The Core Temperature Correction Ratio (CTCR) defined in this work tries to compensate this by informing the numerical value of a generic matrix element $a_{ij}$, based on the pair of temperatures $(T_i,T_j)$ through a correction ratio $\hat r_{ij}$.
This correction is calibrated from two additional, directly simulated FMs, defined with ``Dirac temperature distributions'', shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dirac_distributions}.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=140mm]{figures/fig_temp_dist_dirac1000.png}
  \caption{The two additional cases \revA{with} ``Dirac temperature distributions'', used to calibrate the CTCR.}
  \label{fig:dirac_distributions}
\end{figure}


Because the correction must handle both a locally \emph{hot} cell embedded in a colder surrounding and the reverse case, both Dirac FMs of Fig.~\ref{fig:dirac_distributions} are used: one with the central assembly $C$ driven to the hottest database temperature (1000~K) while the rest of the core sits at the coldest temperature (300~K), and one with the assignment reversed. Comparing each Dirac FM against its own locally-interpolated counterpart gives two ratios, each a function of the geometric distance $d$ between $C$ and a generic cell $j$, including $j=C$ itself ($d=0$) (exploiting the hexagonal core's sextant symmetry, so that only $d$, not direction, matters),
\begin{equation}
  r^{+}(d) = \frac{a^{\text{dirac},+}_{Cj}}{a^{\text{interp}}_{Cj}},
  \qquad
  r^{-}(d) = \frac{a^{\text{dirac},-}_{Cj}}{a^{\text{interp}}_{Cj}},
  \label{eqn:ratio_def}
\end{equation}
where the superscript $+$ ($-$) denotes the hot-center (cold-center) Dirac case, and $a^{\text{interp}}_{Cj}$ is the coefficient the ordinary interpolation of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:interp}) would give for that same, uniform-elsewhere temperature field. Each ratio in isolation quantifies how much the local-temperature interpolation assumption misses, in the most extreme, the most spatially sharp case the database can represent; combined, the two make the correction able to handle a temperature difference of either sign.

For an arbitrary temperature field, the correction applied to a generic interpolated coefficient $a^{\text{interp}}_{kl}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \hat r_{kl}(\Delta T_{kl}, d) = \frac{\Delta T_{kl}}{\Delta T_{\text{dirac}}} \left( r^{\pm}(d) - 1 \right) + 1,
  \qquad
  \Delta T_{kl} =
  \begin{cases}
    T_k - T_l, & k \neq l, \\[4pt]
    T_k - \dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(k)|}\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}(k)} T_n, & k = l,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eqn:ctcr}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta T_{\text{dirac}} = 700$~K is the calibration temperature span common to both Dirac cases, and $r^{+}(d)$ is used when $\Delta T_{kl}>0$ (cell $k$ hotter than $l$, or than its own first-ring neighborhood average $\mathcal{N}(k)$ when $k=l$) while $r^{-}(d)$ is used when $\Delta T_{kl}<0$ --- i.e., the calibration case matching the \emph{sign} of the local temperature difference, evaluated at its own geometric distance $d$. For a diagonal coefficient ($k=l$, $d=0$) this reuses $r^{\pm}(0)$ from the same Dirac calibration of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:ratio_def}), so no additional simulation or calibration data is needed to cover this case.
Eq.~(\ref{eqn:ctcr}) thus transfers and scales the calibration ratios $r^{\pm}(d)$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:ratio_def}) to any pair of cells and any temperature field. For $k\neq l$ the scaling is the actual temperature difference between the two cells divided by the calibration span. For $k=l$, source and destination coincide, and $r^{\pm}(0)$ measures how much the raw interpolation misses $a_{kk}$ when the cell is surrounded by neighbors $\mathcal{N}(k)$ at a different average temperature $\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(k)|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}(k)}T_n$; the scaling is then the difference between $T_k$ and that average. 

In this work the two variants compared are referred to as \emph{raw} (interpolation only) and \emph{corrected} (interpolation plus CTCR).


\subsection{Two Sources of Uncertainty for $k_\text{eff}$ and $F$}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

Comparing an interpolated (and CTCR-corrected) result (here generally indicated with $X$) to a direct reference calculation involves two statistically independent Monte Carlo noise sources, which combine in quadrature:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{X,\text{tot}}^2 = \sigma_{X,\text{ref}}^2 + \sigma_{X,\text{interp}}^2 .
  \label{eqn:sigma_tot}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[(a)] \emph{Reference-case noise}, $\sigma_{X,\text{ref}}$: the statistical uncertainty Serpent reports directly for the single reference simulation being compared against. 
  
  \item[(b)] \emph{Interpolated noise}, $\sigma_{X,\text{interp}}$: the noise that the result $X$ inherits from the interpolated (and corrected) matrix, built from the 8 database calculations. This uncertainty source is not available as for (a): it has to be estimated. \revB{For the corrected variant a third, smaller term, the statistical uncertainty of the two calibration calculations, is evaluated separately in Sec.~\ref{sec:res_final}.} % --- the subject of Sec.~\ref{sec:four_methods}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quantification of $\sigma_{X,\text{interp}}$}
\label{sec:four_methods}

In this work four different approaches are introduced to propagate the database uncertainty to the interpolated FM results. Three of these methods share the same procedure: the eight-temperature database is perturbed with random noise, the interpolator and correction of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:ctcr}) are reapplied, and the procedure is repeated many times. The spread of the resulting $k_{\text{eff}}^{\text{interp}}$ and fission source $F^{\text{interp}}$ over these repetitions is the database-side uncertainty. % $\sigma_{\text{database}}$.

\revA{Methods (i)--(iii) below} differ in how the random perturbation is enforced. \revA{The perturbation acts on the fission-matrix coefficients $a_{ij}$ of each database matrix, before interpolation and correction: each realization replaces $a_{ij}$ with $a_{ij}+\delta_{ij}$.} Indicating the perturbation\revA{s} of the retained coefficients \revA{of one database matrix (defined below)} as a vector $\boldsymbol\delta$ drawn from a zero-mean multivariate normal distribution, $\boldsymbol\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol\Sigma)$, the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ factors as
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf{D}\,\boldsymbol\rho\,\mathbf{D},
  \label{eqn:sigma_factor}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{D}$ is the diagonal matrix of the per-coefficient standard deviations reported by Serpent, and $\boldsymbol\rho$ is the correlation matrix. The amplitude $\mathbf{D}$ is common to \revA{methods (i)--(iii)} --- it is read directly from the \revC{Serpent-reported uncertainties of the database} --- so \revA{they} differ only in what they assume for $\boldsymbol\rho$\revA{.}

\revA{$\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is defined separately at each database temperature. It is a $p\times p$ matrix, where $p=6539$ is the number of retained coefficients out of $N^2=16\,129$: coefficients smaller than $10^{-4}$ times the largest element of the 300~K matrix are discarded, and the same $p$ positions are used at all temperatures (the discarded coefficients carry less than 0.04\% of the matrix sum and keep their nominal values). No coupling between temperatures is modeled (see below), so the joint covariance of the eight matrices is the $8p\times8p$ block-diagonal matrix with the eight $\boldsymbol\Sigma(T)$ along its diagonal, which never needs to be assembled.}

Operationally, \revA{for methods (i) and (ii)} the perturbation is drawn column by column. Because the covariance structure derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:analytical_derivation} couples only coefficients sharing a source column, $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is block diagonal, with one \revA{$p_j\times p_j$} block per source region $j$\revA{, where $p_j$ (between 41 and 67) is the number of retained coefficients in column $j$}. Factorizing the dimensionless correlation block as $\boldsymbol\rho_j=\mathbf{C}_j \mathbf{C}_j^{\!\top}$ by Cholesky decomposition, the $m$-th realization of the perturbation of column $j$ at database temperature $T$ is 
\begin{equation}   \boldsymbol\delta^{(m)}_{:,j}(T) = \mathbf{D}_j(T)\,\mathbf{C}_j(T)\,\mathbf{z}, \qquad \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{\revA{p_j}}),  
\label{eqn:cholesky_draw}
\end{equation}

so that $\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\delta_{:,j})=\mathbf{D}_j \boldsymbol\rho_j\mathbf{D}_j$ by construction. A single vector $\mathbf{z}$ is shared by all coefficients of the same column, which is what imposes the correlation; the marginal standard deviation of each individual coefficient remains $\sigma_{ij}$ regardless of $\boldsymbol\rho$. The factorizations are performed once and reused across all realizations. \revA{For method (iii), whose measured correlation matrix also couples different columns, the full $p\times p$ matrix is factorized and the perturbation of all coefficients is drawn at once.} \revA{The eight database matrices come from separate Monte Carlo calculations, in which the fission matrix is tallied directly in continuous energy, and no cross-section library generated by Monte Carlo is shared between them. Their noise is expected to be uncorrelated, and the brute-force replicas bear this out: over the 150 replicas available at each temperature, the correlation coefficient between the values of the same coefficient at two adjacent temperatures, averaged over all retained coefficients, lies between $-0.0007$ and $+0.0013$, consistent with zero. The} perturbations \revA{of the eight matrices} are \revA{therefore} drawn independently, and no inter-temperature covariance is modeled.

Each perturbed database is then passed through the full pipeline: interpolation of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:interp}), correction of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:ctcr}), and solution of the eigenproblem of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:fm_eigen}), yielding one realization of $k_{\text{eff}}^{\text{interp}}$ and of the normalized fission source. The procedure is repeated $M=500$ times, and $\sigma_{\text{interp}}$ is taken as the standard deviation of the resulting ensemble. Propagating the database covariance through a linearized sensitivity expression would be cheaper, but \revC{$k_{\text{eff}}$ and the fission source are nonlinear functions of the database coefficients, through the eigenvalue problem;} resampling the full pipeline avoids any linearization of this dependence. \revC{The correction ratios $r^{\pm}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:ratio_def}) are computed once and kept fixed in all realizations.} \revB{Their statistical uncertainty, which comes from the two calibration calculations, is therefore not part of $\sigma_{\text{interp}}$; it is evaluated separately in Sec.~\ref{sec:res_final}.}

\revA{The four approaches are listed below. Methods (i)--(iii) follow the resampling procedure just described and differ only in the correlation matrix $\boldsymbol\rho$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:sigma_factor}): (i) neglects the correlation, (ii) predicts it in closed form (Sec.~\ref{sec:analytical_derivation}), and (iii) measures it from a single run. Method (iv) does not use Eq.~(\ref{eqn:sigma_factor}): it rebuilds the whole database from independent replicas, and is the reference against which (i)--(iii) are validated in Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}.}

\begin{enumerate}
      \item[(i)] \textbf{Uncorrelated resampling ($\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf{I}$).} Each retained coefficient is perturbed independently using only its own MC-reported variance, $a_{ij}\to a_{ij}+\delta_{ij}$ with $\delta_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{ij}^2)$. This is equivalent to setting $\boldsymbol\rho$ to the identity matrix and is \revA{one of the two assumptions considered in} prior FM uncertainty-quantification work~\cite{HeWaltersNSE2022}. It is the baseline against which the effect of modeling the correlation is measured.

    \item[(ii)] \textbf{Analytical model ($\boldsymbol\rho=\boldsymbol\rho_{\text{pred}}$).} The correlation matrix is predicted in closed form, $\boldsymbol\rho_{\text{pred}}$, derived directly from the branching structure of the fission process. To the best of our knowledge, this closed-form prediction of the fission-matrix correlation structure from the branching process, and its use to build the interpolated matrix uncertainty, has not been reported before: \revA{prior work~\cite{HeWaltersNSE2022} considered two limiting cases, no correlation between coefficients and a full positive correlation within each column, whereas the derivation of Sec.~\ref{sec:analytical_derivation} predicts that coefficients sharing a column are negatively correlated.} The approach requires no Monte Carlo replicas beyond the single nominal database already needed for interpolation.

    \item[(iii)] \textbf{Correlated resampling ($\boldsymbol\rho=\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}$).} The correlation matrix is instead obtained from the cycle-by-cycle tallies of a single MC run, treating each active cycle as a pseudo-replica of the same underlying random vector. Since Serpent does not provide the fission matrix on a per-cycle basis, this required a modification of the source code to tally and print each active cycle's fission matrix separately. \revC{Because the run has 500 active cycles, fewer than the $p=6539$ coefficients, the sample correlation matrix is singular; it is regularized with Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage~\cite{LedoitWolf2004}.}

  This $\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}$ is measured once, at 300~K, and reused unchanged at every other database temperature. \revA{This choice is tested in Sec.~\ref{sec:t_invariance}.}

  \item[(iv)] \textbf{Brute force.} The full eight-temperature database is rebuilt from scratch $N=150$ times, with one independent MC replica per temperature and per repetition, reapplying the interpolator and \revB{the same} correction each time. This bypasses the $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol\rho\mathbf{D}$ construction entirely: all correlations are present in the replicas by construction, with no modeling assumption. It is the most direct and most expensive estimate.
\end{enumerate}

This is the most direct and most expensive estimate, used here as the reference against which (i)--(iii) are validated (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Deriving the Analytical Correlation Model}
\label{sec:analytical_derivation}

Coefficients tallied within a single Monte Carlo criticality calculation are not, in general, statistically independent, because the number of neutron histories simulated per active cycle is fixed, a fact responsible for well-documented biases and correlations in cycle-based Monte Carlo estimators~\cite{BrissendenGarlick1986}. For two FM coefficients sharing the same source column $j$ (i.e., $a_{ij}$ and $a_{lj}$, $i\neq l$), this mechanism can be made explicit and turned into a closed-form prediction.

\revA{The derivation rests on how histories are tracked in the present calculations. Capture and fission are analog, no splitting or weight windows are used, and a history is terminated when it causes fission. The only non-analog element is Serpent's default implicit treatment of $(n,xn)$ reactions, which changes the weight of a neutron but not the number of histories; its effect is quantified after Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred_nu}). Two properties follow: a history contributes to at most one destination cell, and different histories of the same cycle are independent of one another given the source distribution, since simulated particles do not interact. Under implicit capture or implicit fission, where a history survives an absorption or a fission and may fission again, or under particle splitting, where the descendants of one source neutron are not independent, these two properties would have to be revisited.}

At the start of a cycle, the number of source neutrons born in column $j$, $N_j$, is fixed and inherited from the fission bank of the previous cycle. Each of these $N_j$ histories \revA{ends in exactly one of $N+1$ mutually exclusive outcomes: a fission in one of the $N$ cells, or no fission at all (capture or leakage). If $\pi_i$ is the probability that a history causes fission in cell $i$, the probability that it ends in any other way is $1-\pi_i$. The outcome of a single history therefore follows a categorical distribution, and the numbers of histories ending in each outcome follow a multinomial distribution.}

% NOTA NOTA
% (the same probability regardless of which of the $N_j$ histories is considered, since they are all born under identical conditions in column $j$)


If \revA{a history causes} fission, all $\bar\nu$ secondaries are born together at that one destination. Writing the contribution of history $s$ to destination $i$ as $X_s^{(i)} = \bar\nu\,I_s(i)$, where $I_s(i)$ is $1$ if history $s$ fissions in $i$ and $0$ otherwise \revA{(a Bernoulli variable with mean $\pi_i$)}, the coefficient $a_{ij}$ is the per-source-neutron average:
\begin{equation}
  a_{ij} = \frac{1}{N_j}\sum_{s=1}^{N_j} X_s^{(i)} .
  \label{eqn:aij_def}
\end{equation}

\revA{Since a history fissions at most once, $I_s(i)\cdot I_s(l)=0$ for $i\neq l$. Using the definitions $\operatorname{Var}(U)=E[U^2]-E[U]^2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=E[UV]-E[U]E[V]$, where $E[\cdot]$ is the expected value, and $E[I_s(i)^2]=E[I_s(i)]=\pi_i$ (an indicator equals its own square), one obtains} for a single history,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}\left(X_s^{(i)}\right) = \bar\nu^2\,\pi_i(1-\pi_i), \quad
  \operatorname{Cov}\left(X_s^{(i)},X_s^{(l)}\right) = \bar\nu^2\left[\underbrace{\revA{E\big[I_s(i)I_s(l)\big]}}_{=\,0} - \pi_i\pi_l\right] = -\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i\pi_l ,
  \label{eqn:single_history}
\end{equation}
and summing $N_j$ independent, identically distributed histories (thus summing over $s$) and dividing by $N_j$ as in Eq.~(\ref{eqn:aij_def}),
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(a_{ij}) = \frac{\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i(1-\pi_i)}{N_j}, \qquad
  \operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{lj}) = -\frac{\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i\pi_l}{N_j}, \qquad i\neq l .
  \label{eqn:multinomial_cov}
\end{equation}
The covariance is \emph{negative} for every pair of destinations sharing a source column, and this follows directly because a fixed number of histories is split, exhaustively and mutually exclusively, among the possible \revA{outcomes}, so that more of $N_j$ going to $i$ automatically means less going to $l$. Dividing the covariance by the geometric mean of the two variances, the common factor $\bar\nu^2/N_j$ cancels exactly, leaving a correlation that depends only on the destination probabilities themselves, not on $N_j$, which is a Serpent run-time setting, not a physical property of the fission process:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\text{pred}}(i,l) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{lj})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(a_{ij})\operatorname{Var}(a_{lj})}}
  = -\sqrt{\frac{\pi_i}{1-\pi_i}\cdot\frac{\pi_l}{1-\pi_l}} .
  \label{eqn:rho_pi_form}
\end{equation}
\revA{This is the Pearson correlation coefficient: $\rho\in[-1,1]$ measures the strength and the direction of the linear relationship between two variables, and a negative value means that a positive fluctuation of one coefficient tends to be accompanied by a negative fluctuation of the other. Since $\pi_i\ll1$, the predicted correlations are weak: at 300~K their mean over all pairs sharing a column is $-0.0031$, and the strongest is $-0.08$.}
Equation~(\ref{eqn:rho_pi_form}) is written in terms of the destination probabilities $\pi_i$, which are not directly tallied; what Serpent reports is the mean coefficient $a_i = \bar\nu\pi_i$ (the expected number of secondaries reaching $i$ per source neutron, i.e., $\pi_i = -\kappa a_i$ with $\kappa=-1/\bar\nu$). Substituting $\pi_i=-\kappa a_i$ into Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pi_form}) and simplifying gives the closed form used for method (ii),
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\text{pred}}(i,l) = \kappa\,\frac{\sqrt{a_i a_l}}{\sqrt{(1+\kappa a_i)(1+\kappa a_l)}}, \qquad \kappa=-\frac{1}{\bar\nu},
  \label{eqn:rho_pred}
\end{equation}
which requires only $\bar\nu$ (from \texttt{NUBAR} in \texttt{\_res.m}) and the mean values of the nominal database matrix itself, with no additional MC replicas. (The multinomial variance and covariance results used in Eqs.~(\ref{eqn:single_history}-\ref{eqn:multinomial_cov}) are standard; see, e.g., Ref.~\cite{CasellaBerger2002}.)

Two simplifications in this derivation deserve a comment. The multiplicity $\bar\nu$ has been treated as a single constant, whereas $\nu$ depends on the incident neutron energy and each cell sees a slightly different spectrum. Each history has also been given the same weight, whereas Monte Carlo weights are not exactly unity.

Neither affects the amplitude of the perturbation. The matrix $\mathbf{D}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:sigma_factor}) is read from the statistical errors Serpent reports for each coefficient, and those already contain the local spectrum and the weights of the actual simulation. The multiplicity enters only through the conversion $\pi_i=a_i/\bar\nu$ needed to evaluate Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred}), because the common factor $\bar\nu^2/N_j$ cancels in Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pi_form}). The multiplicity entering the tally is also an expected value: Serpent increments the fission-matrix bin by the neutron weight times $\bar\nu(E_0)$, evaluated at the incident energy of the neutron inducing that fission, and the integer number of secondaries sent to the fission bank never enters the score. The form $X_s^{(i)}=\bar\nu\,I_s(i)$ therefore describes the estimator itself, and the variance of $\nu$ around its mean does not contribute to $\operatorname{Var}(a_{ij})$.

Both simplifications enter the derivation at the same place. Neither changes the covariance of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:multinomial_cov}), which vanishes term by term as long as a history fissions once at most, whatever weight and multiplicity it carries; both change only the variance that normalizes it. Writing $c_i^2$ for the relative variance of the per-event score $w\,\bar\nu(E)$, conditional on a fission in $i$, Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pi_form}) becomes
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\text{pred}}(i,l) = -\sqrt{\frac{\pi_i}{1-\pi_i+c_i^2}\cdot\frac{\pi_l}{1-\pi_l+c_l^2}}\,,
  \label{eqn:rho_pred_nu}
\end{equation}
and the model used here is the case $c_i^2=0$. For the present calculations $c^2$ is small. The energy dependence of $\bar\nu$ contributes $1.5\times10^{-4}$, obtained from the eleven-group $\bar\nu$, fission cross section and flux of the reference calculation, and corresponding to a $1.24\%$ RMS spread of $\bar\nu$ across the fission spectrum. The weights contribute a comparable amount, since they depart from unity only through the implicit treatment of $(n,xn)$ reactions, which here accounts for $1.2\times10^{-4}$ of the total source weight. Propagating $c^2=1.5\times10^{-4}$ through the full pipeline changes $\sigma_{\text{interp}}$ by $0.004\%$ on $k_{\text{eff}}$ and by $0.002\%$ on the fission-source RMS. Assigning conservatively the whole $1.24\%$ spread as an error on the $\bar\nu$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred}) changes them by $0.33\%$ and $0.14\%$; for reference, the core-average $\bar\nu$ moves by $0.018\%$ over the whole $300$--$1000$~K database. The aim of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred}) is to provide a justified definition of the correlation rather than an exact model of it, and these effects are well below the accuracy it reaches against the brute-force reconstruction in Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}.

\subsubsection{Uncorrelated Treatment of Coefficients in Different Columns}
\label{sec:cross_column}

The derivation above does not apply to coefficients that do \emph{not} share a column, $a_{ij}$ and $a_{kj'}$ with $j\neq j'$. It would be convenient, but not quite correct, to argue that $N_j$ and $N_{j'}$ are simply independent: the total population $N=\sum_j N_j$ is fixed by the simulation's particle count per cycle, so source regions compete for a shared budget and $\operatorname{Cov}(N_j,N_{j'})\neq0$ in general. This coupling, however, need not propagate to the \emph{normalized} coefficients themselves. By the law of total covariance,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{kj'}) = \underbrace{E\big[\operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{kj'}\mid N_j,N_{j'})\big]}_{\text{(I)}}
  + \underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}\big(E[a_{ij}\mid N_j],\, E[a_{kj'}\mid N_{j'}]\big)}_{\text{(II)}} .
  \label{eqn:total_cov_law}
\end{equation}
where term (I) vanishes because histories born in the disjoint columns $j$ and $j'$ are conditionally independent given $N_j,N_{j'}$ (again, no history-history interaction is assumed). Term (II) vanishes because $E[a_{ij}\mid N_j]=\pi_i$ is, by construction of the normalized estimator of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:aij_def}), a constant that does not depend on the realized value of $N_j$, so its covariance with anything is zero. This rules out one specific channel, competition for a shared per-cycle particle budget, as a source of cross-column correlation; it is not a claim that cross-column correlation is exactly zero for every other conceivable reason (e.g., spatially correlated local flux fluctuations, or the cycle-to-cycle propagation of the fission source itself~\cite{UekiNSE2003}).

\subsubsection{\revA{Check on Independent Replicas}}
\label{sec:replica_check}

\revA{The correlation model is checked directly on the 150 independent replicas of the brute-force set, at each database temperature. The predicted correlations are too weak to be resolved pair by pair, since with 150 replicas a single measured correlation coefficient has a statistical uncertainty of about 0.08, but their effect accumulates in a sum over a column. The standard deviation of each column sum, $\sum_i a_{ij}$ over the retained coefficients, was therefore measured over the replicas and compared with the value predicted from the standard deviations of the individual coefficients, without correlation and with Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred}), as in replica-based checks of Monte Carlo variance~\cite{Brown2009,MervinNSE2013}. The result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:column_sum}. Without correlation the prediction is too large in every column and at every temperature, by about 30\% on average. With Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred}) the points lie along the identity line, and the average over the columns differs from the measurement by about 2\% at most.}

The sign of the within-column correlation is therefore not universal, and the correlation model has to be chosen for the tallying scheme at hand.}

\subsubsection{Temperature Invariance of the Measured Correlation Matrix}
\label{sec:t_invariance}

Method (iii) assumes that the correlation matrix measured at 300~K also applies at the other database temperatures. The simplest test of this assumption is to measure $\boldsymbol\rho$ independently at the opposite end of the database range, 1000~K, and ask whether the two matrices differ by more than the statistical noise inherent in estimating either one.

Differences between correlation matrices are quantified here through the Frobenius norm, which treats a \revC{$p\times p$} matrix as a single vector of its entries and measures its Euclidean length,
\begin{equation}
  \|\mathbf{M}\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{a=1}^{\revC{p}}\sum_{b=1}^{\revC{p}} M_{ab}^2}\,.
  \label{eqn:frobenius_norm}
\end{equation}
The distance between two matrices is the norm of their difference; normalizing by the norm of the reference matrix \revC{$\mathbf{A}$} gives the dimensionless \emph{relative} Frobenius distance used throughout,
\begin{equation}
  d_F(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}) = \frac{\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_F}{\|\revC{\mathbf{A}}\|_F}\,,
  \label{eqn:frobenius_rel}
\end{equation}
so that $d_F=0$ for identical matrices and $d_F\!\sim\!1$ for matrices differing by an amount comparable to their own magnitude.

\revC{For this test each matrix is measured from the active cycles of 50 independent runs at that temperature (25\,000 cycles in all), without shrinkage.} Table~\ref{tab:t_invariance} reports this distance between the 300~K and 1000~K matrices, alongside an internal noise floor for each temperature: the distance between two estimates of the \emph{same} matrix, obtained by splitting that temperature's own cycle data into two halves and measuring $\boldsymbol\rho$ from each. The noise floor is the distance one would obtain even if the true correlation matrix was exactly temperature-invariant, arising purely from the finite statistics of the estimate.

\centering
\caption{Simple Check of Temperature Invariance for the Measured Correlation Matrix}
\label{tab:t_invariance}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\hline
Comparison & Relative Frobenius distance \\ \hline
$\boldsymbol\rho$(300~K) vs.\ $\boldsymbol\rho$(1000~K) & 0.643 \\
$\boldsymbol\rho$(300~K), split-half internal noise floor & 0.909 \\

\revB{Split-half floor $\times 1/\sqrt{2}$ (full sample)} & \revB{0.643} \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\revB{The noise floor is computed from estimates that use half of the data, whose statistical noise is larger by a factor $\sqrt{2}$ than that of the full-sample estimates compared in the first row. Scaled to the full sample, to first order in the noise, it becomes $0.909/\sqrt{2}=0.643$, equal to the distance between the 300~K and 1000~K matrices: the two differ by the amount expected from statistical noise alone, and no temperature dependence is detectable at the resolution of the estimate. The resolution is limited, since distances of 0.6--0.9 mean that the measured matrices are dominated by noise; the test excludes only large changes of the correlation structure with temperature. The analytical model (ii) does not rely on this assumption: $\boldsymbol\rho_{\text{pred}}$ is rebuilt at each database temperature from that temperature's own matrix and $\bar\nu$.}

\revA{The same distance can be evaluated for the two Dirac configurations from which the correction ratios $r^{+}$ and $r^{-}$ are computed, in which the temperature is not uniform. The cycle-by-cycle matrices needed for $\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}$ are not available for these runs, so the comparison is made with the analytical model, which can be evaluated on any matrix. The predicted correlation matrix of each configuration differs from that of the uniform core at the temperature of the surrounding assemblies by $d_F=0.0013$--$0.0014$, the same value obtained by changing only the random seed (Table~\ref{tab:rho_pred_stability}). A single perturbed assembly therefore does not change the predicted correlation structure.}


\subsection{Definition of Error Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Two plain metrics are used throughout, deliberately without a covariance-weighted quadratic form such as a Mahalanobis distance: they are less sensitive to the \emph{spatial pattern} of a residual, but directly interpretable, which is what matters for our case-by-case comparison. For $k_{\text{eff}}$,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta k_{\text{eff}} = k_{\text{eff}}^{\text{interp}} - k_{\text{eff}}^{\text{ref}}, \qquad
  z = \frac{\Delta k_{\text{eff}}}{\sigma_{\text{tot}}} .
  \label{eqn:metrics_keff}
\end{equation}
The fission source $F$ is normalized to unit sum over the 127 cells giving $\tilde{F_i}$. Its local relative deviation in each zone is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \qquad  \delta \tilde{F_i} = \frac{\tilde{F_i}^{\text{interp}} - \tilde{F_i}^{\text{ref}}}{\tilde{F_i}^{\text{ref}}}\times 100\%,
  \label{eqn:metrics_fs_delta}
\end{equation}
and the overall discrepancy between the interpolated and reference source distributions is summarized by the root-mean-square (RMS) of the zone-wise relative deviations,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMS} = \sqrt{\left\langle \delta \tilde{F_i}^2 \right\rangle_i}.
  \label{eqn:metrics_fs}
\end{equation}


Every acceptance criterion in Sec.~\ref{sec:results} is $|z|<2$ with the $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:sigma_tot}); where a figure shows the two terms separately, the reference term is drawn as a $\pm2\sigma_{\text{ref}}$ band and the interpolated term as the cloud of realizations. \revC{$\sigma_{\text{interp}}$} in Eq.~(\ref{eqn:sigma_tot}) uses the analytical model (ii) throughout Sec.~\ref{sec:results}, computationally validated next.

\subsubsection{Stability of the Analytical Model}
\label{sec:stability_of_analytic_model}

It is reasonable to object that the analytical model is not truly independent of Monte Carlo noise: the closed form of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred}) is evaluated on the mean coefficients $a_i$ of a single database, which are themselves affected by statistical uncertainty. If a different random seed were used, the predicted matrix $\boldsymbol\rho_{\text{pred}}$ would differ slightly, and one may ask whether that sensitivity matters.

To test this, $\boldsymbol\rho_{\text{pred}}$ at 300~K was rebuilt from each of the 150 independent seeds of the brute-force set and compared, through the relative Frobenius distance of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:frobenius_rel}), against the matrix built from the production seed (Table~\ref{tab:rho_pred_stability}).

This section presents the same results as the previous one, with a closer look \revC{at} the uncertainty budget and hence at the acceptability of the results.

Combining $\sigma_{\text{ref}}$ with $\sigma_{\text{interp}}$ from the analytical model in quadrature gives the total uncertainty $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:sigma_tot}). Under this combined budget, 75\% of the corrected cases fall within $|z|<2$ (median $|z|=0.94$), against 42\% of the raw cases (median $|z|=2.18$). \revB{Over the ten cases not used to compute the correction ratios, the fractions are 70\% and 50\%, respectively.} $\sigma_{\text{interp}}$ is 14--22~pcm against 9--13~pcm for $\sigma_{\text{ref}}$ in every case, so the database term still dominates the budget and both variants are judged against a relatively wide band. \revB{The statistical uncertainty of the CTCR calibration, not included in $\sigma_{\text{interp}}$, was evaluated by propagating the noise of the two Dirac calculations through the correction: 500 realizations of the two Dirac matrices were drawn with the analytical correlation model and the Serpent-reported uncertainties, the ratios $r^{\pm}$ recomputed from each, and the correction reapplied to the nominal interpolated matrices of the twelve cases. \section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The interpolated and CTCR-corrected fission matrix inherits statistical noise from the database it is built from, but --- unlike a direct MC calculation --- its fundamental eigenvalue and eigenfunction have no uncertainty of their own to read off from any single simulation output; it has to be constructed by resampling the database itself. Of the three cheap ways of doing this, a zero-replica analytical model, derived from the branching structure of the fission process under the simplifying assumption that only same-column coefficients are correlated, reproduces the $k_{\text{eff}}$ uncertainty of \revC{an} $N=150$ brute-force reconstruction at least as well as an approach that requires measuring a full correlation matrix. Both \revC{improve} markedly on assuming no correlation at all. On the fission-source RMS, the \revC{uncorrelated and measured-correlation approaches perform comparably to each other and better than the analytical model, which underestimates it}, though all three remain within the same order of magnitude of brute force. Folding both uncertainty sources into a single budget, the CTCR's point-estimate benefit persists once both noise sources are accounted for, and most of the remaining case-by-case discrepancies are statistically consistent with this combined floor. Where they are not, the residual \revB{is attributed to the local-temperature approximation, which the CTCR corrects only in part, and, where the field contains intermediate temperatures, possibly to the temperature grid,} rather than \revB{to the} fission-matrix noise model used to characterize it --- a distinction the budget makes possible, and a target for future work independent of the covariance itself.

Over all twelve test cases, the mean absolute error of the raw interpolation is 40.8~pcm on $k_{\text{eff}}$ and 1.27\% on the fission-source RMS; with the CTCR it falls to 23.7~pcm and 0.94\%. \revB{Over the ten cases not used to compute the correction ratios, the reductions are from 35.4 to 25.5~pcm and from 1.33\% to 0.94\%.}

\revA{The analytical model assumes that each history contributes to at most one destination, as with the analog capture and fission used here. Non-unit weights only change the normalization of the variance (Eq.~(\ref{eqn:rho_pred_nu})), but variance-reduction techniques such as implicit capture, implicit fission or particle splitting let one history, or its descendants, contribute to several destinations, and change the covariance structure. Extending the model to these schemes\revB{, and to correlations between coefficients of different columns,} is left to future work; where a closed form is not available, the cycle-based estimate of method (iii) remains applicable.}
